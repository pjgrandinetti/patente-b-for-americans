Accident Prevention and First Aid in Italy

Defensive driving, what the law requires at the scene of a crash, how to protect the scene, and the first aid you may and may not give.

The map for this topic

Crashes are prevented in three places: before you drive (a sound vehicle, getting in and out safely), on the road in difficult conditions (rain, snow, fog), and, because prevention sometimes fails, at the crash scene, where the law tells you exactly what to do. Two-wheelers carry their own risk list. The recurring theme is defensive driving: adjust to conditions and anticipate trouble.

Causes of accidents

  • Road-structure factors that can contribute include narrow stretches and bottlenecks that are not signed, unmarked intersections, missing road markings, and a deformed or slippery surface.
  • Things like separate carriageways, lanes, one-way carriageways, guardrails, traffic lights, and flat straight roads without dangerous curves are not causes.
  • The main human causes of rear-end collisions are excessive speed, inattention, and not keeping a safe distance. Neither a failed parking brake nor power steering has anything to do with them.

Before you drive

Vehicle care: no do-it-yourself tuning

  • Unbalanced brakes must be serviced, not "corrected" at the steering wheel.
  • Specialist jobs like the injection pump belong to a mechanic, not to the driver.
  • It is not permitted to boost performance by increasing engine displacement or remapping the fuel-injection ECU. Both are unauthorized modifications, and the same goes for a light quadricycle: no bigger engine, no swapped engine, no exhaust that lifts it past its speed limit.
  • A red oil light that stays on signals a fault, never correct operation.
  • On a slippery road, braking grip is not won by flooring the brake pedal. The wheels lock.
  • The distance covered during the reaction time grows with speed, since the vehicle keeps moving at that speed while you react.

Getting in and out safely

  • Let passengers get in or out only when the vehicle is fully stopped.
  • They should use the sidewalk side, or, if there is none, the side away from traffic.
  • Open a door only when it endangers no one. Never assume the right-hand door is risk-free.
  • Have an adult supervise children getting in or out.

Driving the conditions

Rain and hydroplaning

  • In rain, slow down and increase your safety distance.
  • Keep the wipers going and prevent the windows from fogging.
  • Steer gently, and do not brake hard.
  • Do not speed through puddles, and leave the clutch, the hazard lights and the radiator cover alone: none of them belongs to driving in rain.
  • Hydroplaning (Europeans say aquaplaning for the same thing) is when the tires ride up on a film of water and lose grip: it reduces adhesion and increases skidding, never the reverse.
  • It happens more easily at higher speed, with worn tires, and in lighter vehicles, and it depends on tread depth and pattern. The road's side slope is not among its causes.

Snow and ice

  • On snow, fit winter tires on all wheels or chains on the driven wheels.
  • On snow, moderate your speed and lengthen the distance.
  • On snow, avoid abrupt steering, acceleration, and braking, which means no full throttle when pulling away, no swerving around a stopped vehicle, and no stamping on the brake in a skid.
  • On ice, go slowly and keep well back, three or four times the usual distance.
  • On ice, keep away from the path of oncoming vehicles, and never brake hard when you meet one.
  • On ice, use low gears downhill, and use all-wheel drive if fitted.
  • On ice, avoid sudden acceleration too, as on snow.
  • On ice, take extra care in shaded patches, where it lingers.
  • On ice, never coast in neutral, which cuts the wheels off from the engine, and never hug the barrier.
  • Chains, on the driven wheels, are the equipment for ice. Studded tires on the front wheels are not.
  • Grip on snow is better than on ice.
  • If stuck in snow or sand, put something that gives friction under the slipping wheel, such as mats or wood.
  • To pull away when stuck, if first gear does not do it, try a higher gear, and never full throttle.
  • Where chains are required and it is snowing, two-wheelers may not be on the road at all.

Fog and tunnels

  • In dense fog, drive at a speed matched to visibility, with maximum care and concentration.
  • Be guided by the road markings.
  • Switch on the fog lights or, failing those, the low beams, and the rear fog light. Position lights alone are not enough.
  • Do not tailgate the vehicle ahead, zigzag to be noticed, sound the horn continuously, or leave the left turn signal on.
  • Keep the seatbelt fastened. Unbuckling to be ready to jump out is not a precaution.
  • If you must stop, do so off the carriageway. Stop on it only for force majeure, and then use the hazard lights.
  • In a long tunnel (galleria), increase your distance in a queue so that fire cannot spread between vehicles.
  • In case of fire, abandon the vehicle quickly and follow the signage to the escape routes and the refuges behind fire doors.
  • Follow the spoken instructions of the staff or firefighters. Their orders prevail over the signs, so you do not walk the wrong way.
  • If your own vehicle catches fire and there is no extinguisher, throw sand or earth at the base of the flames or use a wet blanket. Never try to drain the fuel or LPG tank.

Riding two-wheelers

Motorcycles and mopeds have a small frontal profile, so they are hard to see at a distance.

  • Riders should keep the low beam on by day, and at night the low beam, not the high beam, whatever their size.
  • Riders should wear visible clothing. The clothing does affect how well they are seen. Being noisier does not make them more visible.
  • Car drivers must watch for two-wheelers in general, since they are less easily seen, and especially when opening doors.
  • At intersections, expect a two-wheeler to arrive unseen after passing the stopped vehicles, and in a queue expect one filtering past on the right before you open a door.
  • Leave a good lateral gap when passing a two-wheeler, the more so at speed.
  • In town the horn stays for real danger, for riders as for everyone: approaching an intersection is not a reason to sound it.

Certain road features are slippery and dangerous for two-wheelers, especially when wet.

  • These include tram rails (rotaie del tram), manhole covers (tombini), and painted pedestrian crossings. They are a danger for every two-wheeler, whatever its gearbox.
  • Braking on them when wet is particularly dangerous, and high-speed passage over them can cost grip and steering and cause a fall.
  • Cross them straight and upright at steady speed. Do not brake or accelerate sharply, stand on the pegs, or lift the front wheel.
  • Do not ride along the groove of a tram rail.

At a crash scene

  • A driver involved in a crash must stop and assist any injured, calling 112 (the European equivalent of the US 911) when anyone is hurt.
  • Exchange details, meaning personal data, license, registration, and insurance, with the people who suffered damage.
  • Protect the scene so the evidence is not altered, if the dynamics of the crash may need reconstructing.
  • The insurance claim form may be used: signed by every driver involved it speeds up the payout, and passengers can serve as witnesses.
  • With no one hurt, first clear the carriageway, then note the other driver's name, license number, plate, insurer and policy number.
  • Call the police if traffic cannot be restored, if the other driver cannot prove insurance, or if a crime is involved (a drunk driver). If a vehicle drives off, note its plate and type. If you hit a parked car with nobody there, trace the owner or leave your details.
  • With someone hurt, do not move the injured unless imminent danger (fire, traffic) leaves no choice, keep onlookers away, and never pull off a motorcyclist's helmet, but open the visor.
  • The duty to assist covers animals too, and leaving the scene of a crash with injuries can mean arrest.
  • You are not required to drape the car or to move it onto the sidewalk.
  • The warning triangle marks the stopped vehicle where required, chiefly outside towns, and it is not mandatory in built-up areas.
  • Where the triangle is required, it is required day and night, not only at night.
  • The triangle goes on the road behind the vehicle, never on the rear window.

Summary of key points

  1. Adjust to conditions. Most rear-end collisions come from speed, inattention, and too little distance.
  2. In rain, go slow, leave more distance, and use gentle inputs. Hydroplaning worsens with speed, worn tires, and light vehicles.
  3. On snow, use winter tires or chains and smooth inputs. On ice, use a low gear downhill and watch shaded patches. Snow grips better than ice.
  4. In fog, match your speed to visibility, use fog or low beams plus the rear fog light, and do not tailgate. In a tunnel fire, leave the vehicle and follow the signage and staff.
  5. At a crash, stop, assist the injured, exchange details, and protect the scene. The triangle is not required in built-up areas.
  6. Two-wheelers are hard to see, so riders use a low beam by day and bright clothing. Tram rails, manholes, and painted crossings are slippery when wet, so cross them straight and steady.
