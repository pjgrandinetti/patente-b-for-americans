Vehicle Parts and Maintenance for Safety

Tires, brakes, steering, suspension and lights: what each does, how it fails, and the checks the driver is responsible for.

What this topic covers

This topic covers the parts of the vehicle that keep you safe and how to maintain them. It includes grip and road-holding, brakes (and ABS, the anti-lock braking system), tires, suspension and shock absorbers, steering, and the exhaust, plus a few motorcycle-specific checks. Many questions are about cause and effect, meaning what worn or wrong components do to the vehicle's behavior.

Grip and road-holding

  • Grip (adhesion) lets the wheels drive, steer, and hold a line. It also opposes the tire's sliding on the asphalt, whatever force pushes the tire sideways.
  • Grip is greater on rough, dry, clean asphalt and lower on surfaces that are wet, oily, or marked. Anything that comes between the tire and the asphalt lowers it, leaves, ice, and mud included.
  • When grip falls, the braking distance grows and hard braking becomes dangerous.
  • When grip falls, the wheels can skid, in curves above all, and the drive wheels can spin.
  • Where grip is low, speed must come down, on every kind of road and not only in curves. Low grip also caps the speed at which a curve can be taken safely.
  • Grip is notably lower on tram rails, road markings, and manhole covers when wet, which matters for motorcycles. A motorcycle's grip depends on the type of road surface it is crossing.

Curves: skidding and rollover

  • In a curve a force pushes the vehicle toward the outside, and it grows with speed.
  • Too much speed can cause a skid or a rollover.
  • Rollover is made more likely by a high or unsecured load, a narrow track with the wheels close together, and a tight curve with a small radius.

Brakes and ABS

The three brakes

Italian law distinguishes three brakes. They are three braking functions, not three controls: one slows and stops the vehicle in motion, one stops it when the first fails, and one holds it still when parked. Two words describe how a brake carries its force, and the bullets below use both: a hydraulic brake sends it through fluid in a sealed circuit, while a mechanical one sends it through a rod, a cable or a linkage.

  • The service brake (freno di servizio) is the foot pedal. It slows and stops the vehicle in motion, it is hydraulic, and it is built on two separate circuits, so a single failure disables only part of it.
  • The secondary brake (freno di soccorso) is the braking that remains when part of the service brake fails. It comes in automatically, works through the circuit still intact, acts on at least one pair of wheels, and is therefore weaker than the service brake. It has no control of its own.
  • The parking brake (freno di stazionamento) is mechanical. It holds a stopped vehicle, including in an emergency, and it must hold on a slope.
  • The parking brake is not used to slow a moving vehicle, not even partly engaged on a steep descent. The one exception is a complete failure of the service brake.
  • The parking brake acts on some wheels only, with no balance between them and no ABS, which is why it brakes a moving vehicle badly.
  • Not every parking brake is a hand lever. Some are electric, some are pedal-operated, and some vehicles have none at all, motorcycles among them.

For US drivers. In American usage the parking brake is also called the emergency brake, and the two names refer to one lever. The Italian freno di soccorso is not that lever, despite the similar name. It is the second of the three brakes above, the braking that the intact circuit of the foot brake still provides after a partial failure. A car therefore has three brakes in the legal sense and two controls, the pedal and the parking brake. Where a statement says freno di stazionamento, the American emergency brake is meant. Where it says freno di soccorso, the back-up function of the foot brake is meant.

Using the brakes on the road

  • Engine braking (freno motore) is strongest in first gear, so on a long, steep descent use a low gear and the brakes will not overheat and fade.
  • Never coast in neutral or with the engine off to save fuel. Power brakes and power steering both need the engine, and the brakes left on their own overheat.
  • Brakes work by turning the vehicle's kinetic energy into heat through friction. The linings are the part the heat punishes most, so long repeated braking weakens them and the braking fades.
  • Power brakes (servofreno) are common on light vehicles. They multiply the force of your foot using engine vacuum, so they stop working with the engine off, whatever the ignition key is doing, and the pedal turns rock-hard.
  • With the wheels fully locked the vehicle no longer steers.
  • ABS keeps the wheels from locking, which is what makes the braking safer.
  • A wheel that still turns keeps answering the steering, so ABS lets you correct your line while braking hard.
  • ABS lets the tire use the maximum grip available while braking.
  • ABS acts on every wheel, each time on whichever is about to lock.
  • ABS works on a wet road and with worn tires.
  • When ABS comes in during an emergency stop, keep pressing the pedal firmly rather than easing off.
  • In an emergency stop, never use reverse gear.

Checks and maintenance

  • The brake circuit is hydraulic on cars and on motorcycles alike, and the fluid is what carries the force from the pedal or the lever to the wheels.
  • Check the level of the brake fluid periodically, on every vehicle. The level falls as the pads wear.
  • Test the brakes after washing the vehicle.
  • Brake pads are changed by a workshop, not by the driver.

When braking is weak or pulls to one side

  • Unbalanced or weak braking comes from air or vapor in the hydraulic circuit, worn linings, unevenly worn drums or discs, different tire pressures on the same axle, overheated linings, repeated heavy braking, or a load that is too heavy or badly distributed.
  • Unbalanced brakes show themselves three ways: only the wheels on one side lock, the vehicle swerves toward the wheel that locks first, and the treads wear unevenly.
  • Weak braking is not caused by a worn parking brake, by worn steering parts, by poorly lubricated drums or discs, by clutch-pedal play, by power steering on a small engine, by disc brakes on the rear axle, by winter tires, or by running on methane, and unbalanced brakes never show up as overheated engine coolant.

Tires

  • Check pressure periodically and cold, including the spare.
  • Under-inflation causes edge wear, more friction and higher consumption, heavy steering, and less stability. Tires therefore run coolest at the pressure the manufacturer recommends.
  • Over-inflation wears the center of the tread and reduces grip.
  • Different pressures on the same axle make the car pull, skid in curves, and brake unevenly.
  • Grip and stability depend on the pressure, so set every wheel to the figure the manufacturer recommends, adjusted for the load carried. That figure follows the load, not the material of the rims.
  • A trailer's tires take the pressure set for the trailer's own load, which may differ from the towing vehicle's.
  • Check the sides of the tires as well, where a cut or a bulge can end in a sudden blowout or deflation.
  • A tire-pressure monitoring system (TPMS) adds safety.
  • The maker's table gives a normal figure and a higher laden figure, so the correct pressure depends on the load and the number of people carried. Set the laden figure before a trip with the seats full or the trunk packed, and go back to the normal one afterwards. It does not mean adding air each time a passenger gets in.
  • The pressure for a long journey is the maker's figure for the load carried, the same as for any other trip.
  • Minimum tread depth is 1.6 mm for cars and 1.0 mm for motorcycles. Tires on the same axle must be identical, and the size must match the vehicle's approval.
  • Winter tires (pneumatici invernali) go on all wheels, and they carry no special lower speed limit.
  • Studded tires (pneumatici chiodati) may be used only in the allowed season, and they too go on all wheels, never on two only.
  • Snow chains (catene da neve) must be an approved type. Using non-approved chains is penalized as if you carried none.
  • Chains go on the drive wheels. On a 4x4 that means all four, not the rear pair.
  • Use chains only on a snow-covered road. On snow-free stretches they damage the suspension.
  • A tubeless tire deflates slowly when punctured, so the car holds its direction. A tire with an inner tube goes down fast instead.
  • With a nail in a tubeless tire you may drive on carefully and stop as soon as you can to have it repaired.
  • Leave the nail where it is until the repair. Pulling it out opens the hole and lets the air escape.
  • Have the puncture repaired properly. A sealant can is one way of doing it, not the only one.
  • Tires may be rotated periodically to even out wear.
  • Tires should be replaced after several years even if not worn.
  • Nitrogen-filled tires use the same pressure as air-filled ones.
  • A space-saver spare has a low speed limit, about 80 km/h. Passengers may still be carried while it is fitted.
  • To change a wheel, park on level, stable ground, set the parking brake and a gear, get passengers off the carriageway, loosen the bolts slightly before jacking, never get under the jacked car, and fully tighten after lowering it.
  • The jack goes only at the lifting points the manufacturer marks under the body, close to the wheel being changed. Any other point of the side member is the wrong answer, and so is the point farthest from the wheel.

Suspension and shock absorbers

  • A suspension sits between the frame and the wheel, and every wheel has one, front and rear alike.
  • Suspension keeps the wheels on the road and absorbs the bumps the surface sends up, so it aids both comfort and safety.
  • The suspension works on a straight road as well as in curves, because a rough surface sends up bumps everywhere.
  • By keeping the wheels pressed to the road, suspension and shock absorbers shape how the vehicle answers the steering, so the precision of the steering depends on their condition.
  • Suspension becomes inefficient through wear or after suffering strong impacts. Its elastic parts absorb blows, and heavy ones damage them all the same.
  • Overloading makes the suspension temporarily inefficient, wears it rapidly when it is repeated, and can break it outright.
  • The remedy for an overload is to reduce the load, because raising the tire pressure leaves the excess weight on the suspension and the shock absorbers.
  • A vehicle with inefficient suspension loses road-holding and comfort, and it tires the driver more. The aim of the low beams can go wrong as well. The failure of a single suspension is enough to bring these effects on.
  • Suspension and shock absorbers are replaced when wear or damage calls for it, rather than at a fixed mileage. They need checks and maintenance throughout the vehicle's warranty, and sealed-circuit shock absorbers need them too.
  • The shock absorbers damp the oscillations of the suspension, and by holding the wheel against the road they contribute to road-holding. A vehicle carries suspension and shock absorbers together, each doing its own part.
  • Overloading makes the shock absorbers inefficient and wears them out early, whatever the driving style, because the excess weight itself is what overworks them.
  • The stiffness of the shock absorbers is adjusted in a specialist workshop, with equipment the owner of the vehicle does not have.
  • Worn shock absorbers are replaced with new shock absorbers, which is what restores the suspension's proper working.
  • Worn shock absorbers make the vehicle behave abnormally in braking and above all in curves, and they worsen its road-holding.
  • Worn shock absorbers wear the tire tread unevenly and raise the risk of breaking the suspension springs.
  • Worn shock absorbers let the body go on bouncing after each bump, so ride comfort drops for the passengers.

Steering

  • Check the play in the steering. Excessive play makes the line imprecise.
  • Vibration in the steering suggests the front wheels need balancing.
  • Wheel alignment (toe) affects steering precision.
  • Power steering needs the engine running.
  • If the power steering fails, the steering goes heavy. A failure shows as heavier steering, never as easier steering.
  • Heavy steering can also mean low tire pressure.
  • A puncture pulls the steering toward the deflated wheel.
  • Power steering helps the driver keep control when a tire is punctured.
  • Power steering does not improve braking.
  • Hold the wheel at the sides, at about 9 and 3 on a clock, never low with the hands under the spokes, and remember many steering wheels adjust for height.

Fire, visibility, and the cabin

  • In a vehicle fire, turn the ignition key off to cut the electrical system.
  • With no extinguisher, throw sand or earth at the base of the flames or smother them with a wet blanket.
  • Never try to empty or remove the fuel tank of a burning vehicle, LPG included.
  • The inertial switch (interruttore inerziale) is a safety device fitted to some vehicles. Its name comes from inertia, the tendency of a moving body to keep moving, and a crash is the moment when the vehicle stops abruptly while everything in it is still moving forward. The switch senses that violent deceleration and at once cuts the flow of fuel to the engine. A crash can break fuel lines and cause sparks, and fuel that keeps flowing is what feeds a fire, so the switch reduces the fire risk.
  • Keep the glass clean and transparent, which gives the driver better visibility outside.
  • Check the wiper blades and replace them when they are worn.
  • Keep the washer fluid reservoir topped up.
  • Stickers and hanging objects reduce visibility. Applying adhesive film to the windshield and to the front side windows is prohibited, whether or not the film can be peeled off later.
  • A cracked windshield is replaced, never taped over.
  • Dirt and dust on the outside of the glass call for the washer and wipers rather than the defogger (the demister: warm air that clears condensation from the inside of the glass). Water that the vehicle ahead throws up onto the windshield on a wet road is cleared the same way, with the washer and the wipers.
  • The air conditioning also defrosts the windshield quickly in winter.
  • In the cabin. Keep nothing loose under the driver's seat, switch on the child locks with children aboard, set the navigator before moving off, and use the phone only with hands-free, never to text.

Variable-message signs

  • A variable-message sign, the electronic panel over an autostrada, carries the same legal force as a fixed sign. It is not limited to warning of danger: it can impose an obligation and it can set a prohibition.

The exhaust system

  • The exhaust carries gases away after reducing their toxicity, pressure, and temperature, and it lowers noise.
  • A deteriorated muffler raises noise pollution.
  • A deteriorated muffler is replaced with an approved type for that vehicle.
  • The exhaust is replaced at a workshop.
  • A holed pipe is never patched with glass wool and tape.
  • The exhaust system is not replaced at every oil change.
  • Modifying or tampering with the exhaust system is punishable even in town. A vehicle is driven only with a working muffler, the silenziatore, on roads inside town and outside it alike.
  • Electric vehicles have no exhaust to maintain.

Motorcycle-specific checks

  • On most motorcycles the hands do much of the work that a car driver's feet do. The right hand turns the throttle grip and pulls the front brake lever, and the left hand pulls the clutch lever, while the left foot shifts gears and the right foot works the rear brake. So a rider's hands stay on the handlebar whenever the motorcycle is moving, and also when it is held still on a slope with the brakes.
  • The emergency (kill) switch is a small switch on the handlebar, usually beside the right grip where the thumb can reach it. It stops the engine at once, without taking the hands off the bars. This matters most when you stop on a steep slope, because both hands are holding the brake levers and you cannot let go to reach the key.
  • The switch is also there for a stuck throttle, when the grip stays open after you release it. If twisting the grip back and forth does not free it, the rider uses the switch and pulls in the clutch at the same time, which removes power from the rear wheel, and then leaves the road and stops.
  • The engine starts only with the emergency switch set to ON or Run. If you stopped the engine with the switch, move it back to ON or Run before you try to start again.
  • The emergency switch sits on the handlebar, not on a pedal, the side stand, or the gear lever.
  • The emergency switch is not a parking brake, a wheel lock, or an anti-theft device.
  • For ordinary stops, prefer the ignition key. The key switches the vehicle off and comes out with you, while the emergency switch is meant for stopping the engine quickly without letting go of the bars.
  • If the emergency switch fails, have it repaired promptly.
  • Check the transmission chain's tension, lubrication, and wear. A chain left unlubricated wears quickly and can snap.
  • A chain that snaps can lock the rear wheel and cause loss of control.
  • A worn chain is replaced, never reinforced with wire.
  • Often the front and rear sprockets go with the chain when it is replaced.
  • The transmission chain connects the front sprocket on the engine to the rear sprocket on the wheel, and that link is how the drive reaches the wheel. New sprockets keep the vehicle's original transmission ratio.
  • A motorcycle's engine oil is checked with the bike upright on its wheels, never on the side stand, on level ground, and a few minutes after the engine has been switched off. Upright means vertical, held by hand with both wheels on the ground, because on the side stand the bike leans and the oil gathers on one side.
  • Some motorcycle engines show the oil level through a small window on the sump.
  • A motorcycle has levels to check periodically like any vehicle, which are the engine oil and the brake fluid in its transparent reservoir.
  • An air-cooled motorcycle has no coolant to check. Only a water-cooled one does.

Summary of key points

  1. Grip is higher on rough, dry surfaces and lower when wet or oily, or on rails, markings, and manholes. Low grip means longer braking and skids.
  2. In a curve, the outward force grows with speed. High or unsecured loads, a narrow track, and a tight radius favor rollover.
  3. Use a low gear on long descents to avoid brake fade. With ABS, keep the pedal pressed. Power brakes need the engine, and the parking brake is not for normal braking.
  4. For tires, check them cold, including the spare, with tread of 1.6 mm for a car and 1.0 mm for a motorcycle, identical tires on the same axle, winter tires on all wheels, and approved chains.
  5. Worn suspension or shock absorbers ruin road-holding and wear tires unevenly. Power steering needs the engine, and a puncture pulls toward the flat tire.
  6. The exhaust cuts toxicity, pressure, temperature, and noise. On a motorcycle, check the kill switch, chain, oil (upright), and brake fluid.
  7. In a fire, turn the key off and smother the flames with sand, earth, or a wet blanket. Never touch the fuel tank. Keep the glass clean, replace a cracked windshield, set the navigator before moving, and use the phone only hands-free.
